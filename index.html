MGAT Alpha v3.1: could rule-based trading programs beat simply holding the same coins?

Eight automated programs traded cryptocurrencies on paper, every hour, for eleven weeks, against rules for judging that were written and signed before the first trade. This is the final account of what they did and what it shows.

Summary of results

No strategy family showed a reliable advantage over holding the same coins after fees. The programs lagged holding while prices rose and lost less while prices fell, which is what holding cash does, not what good trade selection does. The experiment was stopped on 9 November 2026 and will not continue with real money.

  • Three independent AI readers, from three different companies, read the frozen records and all recommended ending the experiment.

1The experiment

MGAT tested one question: does an automated trading program earn more than an investor who buys the same coins and holds them? Version 3.1 removed everything except the trading logic. It traded on paper: real market prices, simulated money, no slippage. That made the rules the only thing being tested.

A dedicated computer ran one decision round every hour, day and night. It held $8,000 of paper money in eight programs of $1,000 each: one each for the L1, BC and MED families and five for DEX, one per blockchain network. Each purchase used 15% of a program's available cash (minimum $20, maximum $600), at most three positions per program, with exchange fees of 0.1% to 0.3% charged on every buy and sell.

Two AI advisers recorded an opinion on trades without any power to change them: a simple built-in rule from the start, and from 13 October a large language model (Sonnet).

2Test period and how it was judged

The programs traded from 25 August to 8 November 2026. The operator stopped the run on 9 November at 20:26 UTC, at 139 of the 200 planned trades; the books were frozen and fingerprinted at that moment. The rules for judging were signed before the first trade, so the results could not be read selectively afterwards:

  • The run ends at 200 closed trades, on 30 January 2027, or earlier by the operator.
  • A software repair on 7 October splits the run into segment 1 (before) and segment 2 (after). Each family is judged on segment 2 alone and the two are never pooled.
  • A family passes only with at least 30 trades, a positive average after fees, a positive average even without its two best trades, and a better yearly return than both the Nasdaq-100 and the S&P 500.
  • Every trade must be confirmed by a separate, outside price record.

Reaching 200 trades would have taken about five more weeks, and no family was close enough to the pass line for those trades to change the answer, so the run was stopped early.

3Results

The fleet as a whole

The 139 closed trades booked +$170.52. Four of them, on BNB Chain, took their prices from a single source that could not be cross-checked; one alone booked +$515.83. The signed rules exclude them. The 135 retained trades lost $227.15, an average of −1.4% per trade after fees, with 34% of trades profitable. At the stop the account stood at $8,154.90, including 7 open positions bought for $728.11. The largest fall from a peak was 5.4% as booked, 11.2% without the four unverifiable trades.

Against simply holding the same coins

The pattern is the same in every family, which points to its cause: a program that is out of the market much of the time earns less when prices rise and loses less when they fall. Some of that idle cash was built in, since three of the five DEX programs ($3,000 of the $8,000) never made a trade.

How trades ended

The AI advisers

The built-in adviser approved every trade in three families and rejected every trade in the fourth, so it was a label for the family rather than a judgement. The Sonnet adviser was scored on 50 trades: the 5 it approved lost 5.3% on average and the 45 it rejected lost 5.5%. A permutation test gives p ≈ 0.97, meaning the difference is easily explained by chance. Its questions contained only information available at the time of each trade.

4Limits of the evidence

  • No outside confirmation. The separate price record stored only the latest price, so it confirmed none of the 139 trades. Every figure is the program's own record.
  • Small samples. L1 completed 22 trades and DEX 8. A small genuine advantage could hide in numbers this small.
  • One market cycle. The period held one rise, to early October, and one fall. A sideways market was never tested.
  • Paper is not real. Fees were modelled, but slippage, minimum order sizes and failed orders were not. Real execution would only lower the results.

5Conclusion

On eleven weeks of hourly decisions and 135 retained trades, no MGAT strategy beat holding the same coins. Selecting small coins lost money, the AI adviser had no predictive value, and the one useful behaviour, sitting in cash while prices fell, is a known property of trend-following rules rather than a discovery. The experiment ended on 9 November 2026 and was not moved to real money.

What remains is the method: rules for judging written before the data, every number re-checked by independent readers, and a plain account of what the data cannot show.

Hades: could an AI program left alone with real money beat simply holding?

One AI program held its own Solana wallet with $130 of real money, decided every hour with no human in the loop, and was told the whole market was open. Four AI set-ups from three makers took turns over eleven weeks. This is the final account.

Summary of results

No AI model found an advantage over simply holding SOL. Left free, the most capable model made no trade in eight days. Given a deadline, it made three small, planned trades and finished slightly ahead of holding, but short of the five trades the test asked for. The experiment ended on 9 November 2026.

1The experiment

MGAT tested fixed rules. Hades tested the opposite idea: give an AI program real money and freedom, and see whether it finds and acts on an advantage that fixed rules miss.

  • Money: $130 of real money, 1.3623 SOL, in a dedicated Solana wallet. A one-time top-up of 0.08 SOL was added later.
  • Freedom: internet access, its own wallet, and a wake every hour to decide on its own. No human approved or placed any trade.
  • One rule: the wallet must never reach zero.
  • Benchmark: simply holding the same SOL, measured in SOL and in dollars at the same moment.

2Test period and stages

The experiment ran from 24 August to 9 November 2026. The decision-maker changed in five stages, each one designed from what the previous stage showed. The last two stages were formal tests with a pass line set in advance.

The five stages. Stages 4 and 5 overlap by a day: the stage-5 deadline was given on 4 November and stage 4 was formally closed on 5 November.

Test 1 asked whether the model would trade at all once nothing held it back. Test 2 asked for at least five trades it could explain, and a finish ahead of holding SOL, by 11 November. It reached three trades, so the experiment was ended on 9 November.

3Results

Measured in dollars, the wallet rose to $175.92 in late September and fell to $126.70 by the end, almost exactly in step with SOL itself (about $95 at the start, $121 at the peak, $86.75 at the end). Holding the same SOL, including the top-up, would have ended at $125.11.

What the AI models did

  • DeepSeek traded most, twelve times in six weeks, and ended level with holding.
  • Gemini made two trades in six days, too few to judge.
  • Sonnet, with limits, held for fifteen days. When every limit was lifted it changed its words, not its actions: eight more days, no trade.
  • Sonnet, given a deadline, acted within the hour. Each trade had a stated reason, size, target and stop, written before execution. It finished 0.63% ahead of holding on about $1 of gains, but its first stated reason was the deadline, not the market.

4Limits of the evidence

  • One small wallet. $130 cannot show how an AI program behaves with sums where costs, slippage and risk matter more.
  • One asset in practice. The program only ever traded SOL against a US-dollar token, so the rest of the market was never tested.
  • One market phase. SOL rose into late September and fell through October and November.
  • Changing set-ups. Each stage used a different model or instruction, so no single set-up ran long enough to judge on its own.

5Conclusion

Over 77 days with real money, four AI set-ups from three makers found no advantage over simply holding SOL. Given freedom, the most capable model chose not to trade; given a deadline, it traded small and carefully, which is discipline but not evidence of an edge. The real cost was about $10 plus AI usage fees. The experiment ended on 9 November 2026, the wallet was emptied and retired, and no successor is running.
